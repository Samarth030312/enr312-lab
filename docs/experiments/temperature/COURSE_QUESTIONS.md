# ENR312 TEMP handout: how this set-up answers every task and question

Source: ENR312 *Temperature Control Experiment (TEMP)* guidelines, SRC-015 (not redistributed).
Apparatus: Arduino Uno, **DHT11** (lab sensor), bulb in a holder, solid-state relay, 16x2 LCD
(parallel or I2C, not yet identified), wiring in [`TEMPERATURE_WIRING.md`](../../wiring/TEMPERATURE_WIRING.md).
Desired temperatures: 35, 45, 55 °C.

Status: **DONE-SIM** simulation answers ([`COURSE_SIM_RESULTS.md`](COURSE_SIM_RESULTS.md), generated by
`matlab/scripts/temperature_course_questions.m`), **PENDING-HW** needs the rig (`enr312_start temperature`:
connect, choose the mission and preset, RUN ON RIG). The thermal constants are ESTIMATED until the heater-step run is
made; then rerun the script with the fitted K, τ, θ (`enr312.temperature.withFOPDT`).

**Mains safety first:** the bulb runs on 230 V AC. Wire it unplugged, inside a closed enclosure, SSR
load side only; the Arduino side of the SSR is low voltage. See `docs/safety/ELECTRICAL_SAFETY.md`.

## Section 2 — questions (reference answers)

1. **How does a microcontroller work?** A single chip with CPU, flash (program), RAM, and peripherals
   (timers, ADC, UART, GPIO). After reset it runs the program from flash forever: read inputs, compute,
   write outputs. The Uno's ATmega328P runs at 16 MHz with 32 kB flash and 2 kB RAM.
2. **Where does the board get power?** USB (5 V from the laptop), the barrel jack or VIN (7–12 V through
   the on-board regulator), or a regulated 5 V into the 5V pin (bypasses the regulator; no protection). Three ways.
3. **Microprocessor vs microcontroller.** A microprocessor is only the CPU; memory and peripherals are
   separate chips (PCs). A microcontroller integrates them on one chip for small dedicated control tasks.
4. **DHT11 and LM35.** DHT11: a thermistor and a humidity sensor read by a small internal chip that sends
   a 40-bit digital frame on one data wire (needs a pull-up, ~1 reading/s). Wiring: VCC 5 V, GND, DATA → D7.
   LM35: an analog IC whose output is 10 mV/°C (0 V at 0 °C): VCC 5 V, GND, Vout → A0; T = ADC × 500/1023.
5. **Relay, types.** An electrically operated switch that lets a small signal control a separate circuit.
   Electromechanical (coil moves contacts; SPST/SPDT, latching), reed relays, solid-state (semiconductor, no
   moving parts), and contactors for high currents.
6. **Solid-state and AC-DC relay.** An SSR uses an optocoupler (LED on the input side) to switch a triac or
   thyristors (AC loads) or MOSFETs (DC loads); input and load are electrically isolated. "DC-AC" (e.g.
   SSR-40**DA**) = DC control input (3–32 V) switching an AC load. Most are zero-cross: they turn on only
   when the mains voltage passes 0 V and turn off when the current reaches 0.
7. **LCD.** Liquid crystal cells between polarisers twist light depending on the applied voltage; a
   controller chip (HD44780) drives a 16×2 grid of 5×8-dot characters from commands written to it.
8. **Connecting the LCD.** Parallel 4-bit: RS, E, D4–D7 to six pins (here D12, D11, D5, D4, D3, D2), RW to
   GND, contrast potentiometer on V0, backlight via resistor; library `LiquidCrystal`. With an I2C backpack:
   SDA → A4, SCL → A5 + 5 V/GND; library `LiquidCrystal_I2C`.
9. **"Hello" at six positions.** `lcd.setCursor(col, row)` then `lcd.print("Hello")`, column = 0 (left),
   (16−5)/2 = 5 (centre), 16−5 = 11 (right), row 0 or 1. Sketch: `temperature/examples/lcd_hello_alignment/`.
10. **Board to computer.** USB (type-B on the Uno), carrying power and a virtual serial port (the
    ATmega16U2 USB-to-serial bridge; CH340 on clones) used for uploading and the Serial Monitor.
11. **Where is the temperature seen?** On the LCD, in the Serial Monitor / Serial Plotter (115200 baud,
    `$TTEL` lines), and live in `enr312_temp`.
12. **Moving average, window size.** The output is the mean of the last N samples; N is the window size.
    It smooths noise and delays the signal by (N−1)·Ts/2.
13. **P control on the Uno.** u = Kp·(setpoint − T), clamp 0–255, `analogWrite(9, u)` every Ts.
14. **On-off vs P.** On-off has only two output levels (full on/off); P scales the output with the error.
    On-off for slow, cheap, tolerant processes (ovens, water heaters); P (or PI) where a steady value and
    gentle actuation matter.
15. **Drawback of on-off.** Continuous oscillation and frequent switching (wear, flicker). Reduce with
    hysteresis, a minimum on/off time, filtering, or proportional/time-proportioning control.
16. **Hysteresis.** Different switching points for rising and falling (on below SP − h/2, off above SP + h/2):
    small fluctuations inside the band no longer toggle the output. Mode HYST, band 1 °C (= ±0.5 °C).
17. **PWM.** A square wave whose on-time fraction (duty) sets the average output. The Uno makes it with
    hardware timers: pins 5/6 ~980 Hz, pins 3/9/10/11 ~490 Hz, 8-bit (0–255). Useful for motors, LEDs and
    heaters; with a zero-cross SSR it interacts with the 50 Hz mains (S3-6).
18. **Sampling time.** How often the controller measures and acts. Too long: slow reaction, extra delay,
    possible instability, missed changes; too short: wasted effort, more noise sensitivity, and for slow
    sensors (DHT11, 1 s) no new information.
19. **SSR on AC.** Input current lights an LED that triggers a photo-triac/thyristor pair; zero-cross
    circuitry delays turn-on to the next zero crossing; it turns off at the next current zero. Preferred:
    no contacts (silent, no arcing, long life, fast, vibration-proof); downsides: heat, leakage, voltage drop.
20. **ADC.** Converts an analog voltage to a number. The Uno has a 10-bit ADC (0–1023) on A0–A5,
    5 V reference by default (4.9 mV per step). The DHT11 is digital, so it is not used here.
21. **P controller and PID.** P reacts to the present error. I adds the accumulated error (removes
    steady-state error), D reacts to the rate of change (damping, anticipation). P is the base term of PID.
22. **55 °C held at 53–57 °C?** For a bulb heater with a DHT11 (±2 °C, 1 °C steps, rated to 50 °C) this
    is about as good as the sensor can tell; whether it is acceptable depends on the application. Accuracy
    depends on sensor accuracy/resolution/range, sensor placement and lag, dead time, actuator type,
    controller and sampling.
23. **DHT11 vs LM35.** DHT11: digital, 0–50 °C, ±2 °C, 1 °C steps, ≥1 s per reading, slow. LM35: analog,
    −55…150 °C, ±0.5 °C typ., resolution set by the ADC (~0.5 °C on the Uno, better with the 1.1 V
    reference), continuous and faster — better for a 55 °C loop.
24. **Mains safety.** Shock, fire, burns from the bulb. Never touch live parts; wire with the plug out;
    enclosure and insulated terminals; fuse; earth; SSR with heat-sink and margin; keep the Arduino side
    isolated (the SSR does this); one person in charge of the plug.
25. **Applications.** Industrial ovens and plastic moulding, incubators and baby warmers, HVAC/thermostats,
    3-D printer hot-ends, water heaters, sous-vide. Our rig is the same loop in miniature: sensor → controller
    → relay → heater, with the same trade-offs between switching and accuracy.
